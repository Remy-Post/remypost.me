# Colour psychology research for Remy Post's brand-book portfolio

Audience: recruiters / hiring managers for co-op & junior dev roles. Goal: a light-first editorial "brand book" palette that reads competent, sophisticated, warm/human, and distinctive — not another purple-gradient AI-template site.

---

## 1. What the peer-reviewed evidence actually says

**Hue → brand personality mapping (Labrecque & Milne, 2012, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, "Exciting red and competent blue: the importance of color in marketing").** This is the most directly relevant marketing study. It empirically links specific hues to Aaker's brand-personality traits: red associates with excitement, blue with competence, brown with ruggedness, and white/light tones with sincerity. It also shows color choice can shift purchase intent and that it interacts with a brand's familiarity/likability. [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11747-010-0245-y.pdf), [citation details](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1007/s11747-010-0245-y). Caveat: I could not get past the publisher paywall to confirm effect sizes directly, only the framework and widely-cited hue→trait pairings; treat the specific pairings as the paper's reported associations, not universal laws.

**Elliot & Maier's colour-in-context theory, reviewed in Elliot (2015), *Frontiers in Psychology*, "Color and psychological functioning: a review of theoretical and empirical work."** This is the honest, skeptical anchor for the whole brief. Key points, read directly from the paper ([PMC4383146](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383146/)):
- The theory combines biologically-based predispositions with socially-learned associations; context (what color appears on, in what setting) changes what a color signals. It is explicitly a *general* framework, not a precise predictive model — Elliot says its premises are only "vaguely suggestive" of hypotheses in any specific situation.
- Elliot calls the field "nascent" and flags weak theory (narrow or overly broad, almost all about red) and weak empirics (poor color control, uncontrolled viewing conditions, underpowered studies).
- **Hue dominates the literature; lightness and saturation are comparatively under-studied.** Lightness gets some treatment via conceptual-metaphor theory (light = good, dark = bad); chroma/saturation is "almost entirely overlooked," and no body of work isolates hue × lightness × chroma interactions.
- On blue and trust: the review's own summary table reports that blue stores/logos have been shown to raise quality and trustworthiness appraisals across multiple labs — but it is one of a short list of effects meeting a 5-lab-replication bar, and it still sits inside a field the reviewer calls immature.
- On red and excitement: the review traces this to Goethe/Goldstein-era wavelength theorizing (warm, long-wavelength colors ↔ arousal), not to a hard modern experimental finding — "exciting red" is a title/heuristic, not an established effect in Elliot's own review.
- No direct evidence on color and *competence* judgments specifically — competence appears only as a hypothetical illustration of the theory, not a tested result.

**Takeaway: be skeptical of pop-psychology "blue = trust, red = excitement, green = growth" lists.** The academic base for simple one-hue-one-meaning claims is thin, inconsistent on moderators, and dominated by red/blue. Decisions here should lean more on things with stronger support — lightness/saturation, context/fit, and distinctiveness — than on hue folklore.

**Aaker's brand personality dimensions (Aaker, 1997, *Journal of Marketing Research*).** Five independent-ish dimensions: Sincerity (honest, down-to-earth), Excitement (daring, trend-forward), Competence (reliable, capable, intelligent), Sophistication (glamorous, premium, polished), Ruggedness (tough, outdoorsy). A later meta-analysis found these five load heavily on one general brand-personality factor (so they're correlated, not fully orthogonal). I found no paper that directly crosses Aaker's five dimensions against hue/saturation/lightness in a unified model — the Labrecque & Milne hue-trait pairings are the closest bridge, and they map onto Aaker's language (red→excitement, blue→competence, brown→ruggedness, white→sincerity). Sophistication is the dimension most consistently tied, elsewhere in the literature, to **lower saturation and darker/more restrained palettes** rather than to any one hue (see §2).

**Singh (2006), *Management Decision*, "Impact of color on marketing."** This is a literature-review/opinion piece, not a new experiment, and it's the source of the viral "90 seconds / 62–90%" claim: people form judgments of people or products within 90 seconds, and color accounts for 62–90% of that snap assessment. The paper states this as a summary of prior claims in its opening lines, not as a result it measured itself. The range has been flattened in later marketing-blog retellings into a single confident number ("90% of first impressions are about color"), which is **not supported by the original source** — it's a reviewer's broad range, uncited back to any single rigorous experiment. **Treat the 90-second/90% stat as a soft, overstated claim — directionally plausible (color is a fast, salient first-impression cue) but not a precise, reliable statistic.**

**Bottomley & Doyle (2006), *Marketing Theory*, "The interactive effects of colors and products on perceptions of brand logo appropriateness."** Core idea: color "works" for a brand largely through *fit* with category expectations, not absolute hue meaning — colors that feel appropriate for a product/category raise liking and credibility; colors that clash with category norms, even if "nice" colors in the abstract, can hurt perception. A related 2018 paper on category color norms frames two ways brands meet expectations: copy the category leader, or lean on the color's intuitive fit (white for dairy, blue for insurance/trust-coded categories). This is the theoretical hinge for the "distinctiveness vs. competence" trade-off in §4 below: you can safely differ from category norms *as long as the deviation doesn't read as incongruent* with what the category needs to signal (reliability, craft, legibility). [Cardiff ORCA record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/2579).

**Color and professional/hiring evaluation specifically.** This is the weakest-evidenced section of the brief, and I want to be direct about that:
- I could not find a rigorous, well-cited peer-reviewed study on resume color or interview-attire color and hiring/competence outcomes. The one plausible academic hit (a Springer article on colors/layering in business attire and perceived competence) returned only a title snippet; I was not able to confirm its method, sample, or findings, so I'm not citing specific effects from it.
- Widely circulated claims ("red increases perceived competence," "68% of hiring managers subconsciously judge by attire color") trace to SEO-style career-advice blogs with no verifiable underlying study. I'm flagging these as **unsupported** rather than citing them.
- The consistent, if informal, theme across professional-dress guidance is that **darker, more muted/desaturated tones read as serious and competent, while brighter/more saturated colors read as less conventional** — this lines up with the better-supported saturation/lightness research in §2, even though the hiring-specific literature itself is thin.
- **Conclusion for this brief:** don't lean on hiring-specific color studies (they don't really exist at rigor), lean on (a) the general saturation/lightness-sophistication literature, and (b) Fiske's warmth/competence framework as a *conceptual* bridge, explicitly flagged as an extrapolation, not a tested finding about personal branding.

**Fiske's Stereotype Content Model (Fiske, Cuddy, Glick & Xu, 2002) and "Brands as Intentional Agents" (Kervyn, Fiske & Malone, 2012).** SCM's two primary social-perception dimensions are **warmth** (good/bad intent) and **competence** (ability to act on intent) — judged first and fastest in person perception, and the dimensions people use to read *brands* too, per Kervyn et al.'s extension. I found no paper applying SCM directly to an individual's personal-brand self-presentation (portfolio sites, resumes) — applying it to Remy's site is a reasonable **inference**, not a cited finding: a junior-developer portfolio that reads as competent-only (cold, sterile, hyper-technical) risks the "competent but cold" quadrant SCM associates with envy/distance; warmth cues (editorial voice, approachable type, a human accent color, personality in the "brand book" framing) should sit alongside the competence cues (precision grid, restrained palette, clear hierarchy) so the site lands in the high-warmth/high-competence quadrant SCM links to admiration/trust. [Fiske et al. 2002 PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/Fiske-et-al-2002.pdf).

## 2. Lightness, saturation and contrast — often doing more work than hue

This is the best-supported, most actionable part of the research, and it should drive the palette more than hue folklore does:

- **Desaturated + darker reads as premium/sophisticated.** A multi-study paper (reported via several secondary sources; original appears to be recent *Journal of Consumer Research*-adjacent work on color saturation and luxury status) found **less-saturated colors raise perceived brand status**, mediated by a sense of heritage/continuity — and the effect reverses when a brand frames itself as new/innovative rather than established. A separate thesis on logo color found **high-saturation logo colors hurt pleasure/luxury perception**, while **low-value (darker) logo colors increased perceived luxury, arousal and dominance**. A 2015–2025 review of ~30 studies on "quiet luxury" explicitly ties desaturated, chromatically subtle palettes to perceived authenticity and timelessness.
- **Elliot's review (§1) independently flags that chroma/saturation is understudied relative to hue** — meaning the strongest *quantified* color-psychology claims actually concern this axis, even though it gets less popular-press attention than "what does blue mean."
- **Practical implication for this brief:** a muted, lower-chroma, deeper-value accent (e.g., a burnt terracotta rather than a bright red-orange; a deep ochre rather than a bright gold) will read as more premium/competent than the same hue at high saturation. High-chroma, high-lightness color (neon-on-black, saturated indigo-purple gradients) is exactly the category-norm "generic AI template" look the brief wants to avoid — and the research gives a concrete reason why it reads as cheap/trend-chasing rather than sophisticated: saturation, not hue, is the main lever for that perception.
- **Warm off-white vs. pure white, near-black vs. pure black.** No single peer-reviewed paper nails this down precisely, but it follows directly from the same lightness/saturation literature plus standard editorial-design practice: pure `#FFFFFF`/`#000000` read as stark, screen-native, and generic (the "default Figma/Tailwind" look); a warm, very-slightly-tinted off-white background and a near-black (not true-black) ink read as intentional, printed, "designed" — consistent with the "quiet luxury"/heritage framing above, and avoids the harsh literal contrast that makes long-form editorial reading fatiguing.

## 3. Colour in hiring / professional-evaluation contexts

Covered in §1. Summary: direct peer-reviewed evidence on color-and-hiring outcomes is essentially absent or unverifiable from what I could access; the informal professional-dress consensus (muted/dark = serious) is consistent with, but not independently proven by, the saturation/lightness research in §2. The more defensible move is to use **Fiske's warmth-competence framework as a design heuristic** (aim for both, not just competence) rather than cite specific hiring statistics.

## 4. Distinctiveness — the branding argument for owning an off-norm colour

- **Romaniuk's distinctive-brand-assets research** (summarized via multiple secondary sources on her book *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* and related work) found that when brand assets are tested for being both *unique to the brand* and *famous/recognized*, **colour is typically the weakest-performing asset type** — characters, logos and fonts outperform color at being simultaneously unique and recognized. Her practical argument is to stop debating "which color" in the abstract and instead ask whether a color can realistically be owned — **a color already used heavily across a category is a poor distinctiveness bet**, almost by definition, regardless of its abstract psychological connotation.
- **Sharp's distinctiveness vs. differentiation framing:** distinctiveness builds mental availability through memorable, consistently-used cues; differentiation requires genuine functional superiority and is much harder to sustain. For a personal portfolio site, the achievable goal is distinctiveness (a consistent, ownable visual signature across the "brand book"), not differentiation (claiming to be functionally better than other junior devs).
- **Category norm for developer portfolios / tech, observed directly from current template marketplaces (Framer, Webflow, converge.ai listings):** the recurring look is a **dark background with a purple-to-indigo radial gradient hero, neon/glow accents, and glassmorphism** — vendors explicitly market this as feeling "familiar to engineers" because it echoes IDE dark themes. This is exactly the look the brief wants to avoid, and it's also, per Romaniuk's logic, a *bad* distinctiveness bet even on its own terms — it's the most saturated category norm, not an edge case.
- **How to differ without hurting perceived competence:** Bottomley & Doyle's appropriateness/fit research (§1) is the guardrail here — a palette can safely sit outside the category norm as long as it still *reads as fitting the job to be done* (legible, calm, precision-oriented), rather than reading as arbitrary or decorative. A warm, desaturated, editorial palette (off-white paper, near-black ink, one muted accent) signals "I thought about craft and readability" — which is a competence signal in its own right for a developer/branding-minded candidate — while still being visually distinct from the dark-purple-neon norm.

## 5. Accessibility constraints

- **WCAG 2.2 contrast minimums (unchanged from 2.0/2.1):** normal text needs **≥ 4.5:1** against its background (SC 1.4.3); "large text" (≥ 18pt/24px regular, or ≥ 14pt/18.66px bold) needs **≥ 3:1**; non-text UI components (icons, control borders) need **≥ 3:1** against adjacent colors (SC 1.4.11); Level AAA raises normal text to **7:1**. Logos/decorative text are exempt. [W3C Understanding doc](https://www.w3.org/TR/2016/NOTE-UNDERSTANDING-WCAG20-20161007/visual-audio-contrast-contrast.html).
- **Colour-vision deficiency:** red-green deficiency (deuteranopia/deuteranomaly, protanopia/protanomaly) is the most common form (~8% of men, ~0.5% of women of Northern European descent). Practical guardrails pulled from the research: don't rely on a red/green pair as the *only* signal differentiator; blue-green/turquoise often reads as gray to red-green-deficient viewers; blue+orange is commonly cited as a safe, high-distinguishability pairing; established accessible palettes (Okabe–Ito, Wong) are good references; always pair color with a second cue (icon, label, underline) for anything meaningful (status, links, form errors); test with a simulator (Coblis, Color Oracle) before shipping.

## 6. Recommended palettes

Both are light-first, warm-neutral, low-to-moderate chroma, dark-ink-on-light-paper "editorial brand book" systems — deliberately outside the dark/purple/neon/teal category norm. Contrast ratios below are computed directly (WCAG relative-luminance formula), not estimated.

### Palette A — "Clay & Ink" (primary recommendation)

| Role | Hex | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Background (paper) | `#F6F1E7` | warm ivory, not pure white — reads intentional/printed (§2) |
| Surface | `#ECE4D4` | warm linen, for cards/panels |
| Primary ink | `#211D18` | warm near-black (umber-black), not pure black (§2) |
| Secondary ink | `#6B6355` | warm taupe-gray, for captions/metadata |
| Signal/accent | `#BB4D2E` | burnt terracotta — desaturated warm red-orange |
| Accent (text-safe variant) | `#A8441F` | darker terracotta for links/accent body text |
| Second accent | `#2E4034` | deep, desaturated pine green |

**Rationale tied to evidence:**
- Terracotta sits adjacent to the "exciting red" family in Labrecque & Milne's mapping, but pulled down in saturation and lightness — per §2's luxury/saturation research, that shift moves it from "exciting/cheap" toward "warm but composed," landing closer to sincerity + a touch of excitement rather than raw excitement. It's also well outside the blue/purple/teal/neon category norm for dev sites (§4), while still reading as *fitting* (warm, human, crafted) rather than arbitrary (Bottomley & Doyle fit logic).
- The deep pine second accent gives a quiet, desaturated competence/stability note (darker, cooler, restrained — same saturation logic as above) without resorting to generic SaaS blue — useful for success states, secondary links, or a "projects" section accent.
- Warm ivory background + warm near-black ink (not `#FFFFFF`/`#000000`) is the single biggest lever for the "designed, not templated" feeling, per the saturation/lightness literature in §2.
- This palette is built to carry **warmth** (terracotta, warm neutrals) alongside **competence** (high-contrast ink, restrained pine accent, generous whitespace implied by a calm palette) — the two SCM dimensions (§1) a hiring audience reads fastest.

## 7. Bottom line / recommendation

Go with **Palette A, "Clay & Ink,"** for the light mode, and its dark counterpart "Ink & Ember" for dark mode:

- It is the more distinctive of the two relative to the developer-portfolio category norm (terracotta + pine vs. the more "fintech-navy" Palette B, which still echoes — faintly — the blue-competence category default).
- It balances Fiske's warmth and competence dimensions explicitly: warm terracotta + human off-white paper (warmth) against a disciplined near-black ink and desaturated pine accent (competence), rather than leaning on one dimension alone.
- It is grounded in the best-supported part of the color literature (saturation/lightness → sophistication, §2) rather than relying on contested hue-only claims, while still nodding to Labrecque & Milne's directional hue associations where they're used (muted red-orange for warmth/energy, muted green for restraint).
- Every body-text pairing clears WCAG 2.2 AA (4.5:1+), with text-safe accent variants supplied for the one shade (`#BB4D2E`) that's better reserved for large text, icons, and UI chrome.
